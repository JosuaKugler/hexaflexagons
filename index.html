Hexaflexagon Explorer

A hexaflexagon is a hexagon folded from one strip of paper triangles and glued into a loop. Pinch three corners together and it turns inside out, bringing a hidden face to the top. The model below tracks every triangle of the glued strip, so each flex follows the same rules as the paper. Besides the classic pinch flex, the hexahexaflexagon can also do the V-flex, which breaks faces apart and mixes them.

The Tuckerman graph, extended

With pinch flexes alone, the hexahexaflexagon's Tuckerman graph is the face map above: 6 faces and 9 lines, one line for each position. Once V-flexes are allowed, a side can show pieces of several faces, so faces stop working as points. Instead, take every position as a point and join two positions when one flex turns one into the other. The simulation builds that graph here from the starting fold, counting each position once however it is rotated or turned over.

Take away the V-flex links and the graph falls apart into small pieces that pinch flexes alone keep you inside. Two of them are the classic 9-position Tuckerman graph: the flexagon as you fold it, and a second copy whose faces are made of different triangles. Every other piece is a triangle of 3 positions that pinch flexing just goes around, like a trihexaflexagon. In the drawing each piece is one dot, and each line is a V-flex between two pieces.

How many ways out

Most positions allow only the two pinch flexes of their triangle. A V-flex needs three thick enough sectors, so it only becomes possible in some positions, and in just 3 of the 9 positions of each classic copy.

Working it out…

What a pinch flex does

The three marked corners rise and meet above the middle, folding the hexagon into a three-bladed star. The blades then split open from the top and lie flat again. The corners you pinched are now the centre, and the old centre has become three of the outer corners.

Why it sometimes won't open

A blade can only split where its layers are not hinged together. If no such gap exists at the corners you chose, the paper refuses. Move one corner over and try again. The Tuckerman tour does this automatically: flex at the same place while it works, otherwise shift by one corner.

What a V-flex does

Two corners with one corner between them are lifted and pressed together, folding the triangles between them into a fin. The rest of the flexagon becomes a bowl, which is opened, slid into a tent and opened flat again around the joined corners. Choose V-flex and press the button to step through it in 3D; the steps follow the real pieces of paper, which move as rigid triangles except for one layer that has to slip past another. It needs the two sectors on either side of the first corner, and one more, to hold at least two leaves each, so the trihexaflexagon can never do it.

Mixed faces

After a V-flex a side can show triangles from several faces, and the pictures on them can turn. With pinch flexes alone the hexahexaflexagon has 9 arrangements, counting each only once however it is rotated or turned over. Pinch and V-flexes together reach 3,420. The simulation finds exactly that number, as 41,040 states (3,420 times 6 rotations and 2 sides), matching Iacob, McLean and Wang (2012).

How the model is built

The trihexaflexagon is a ring of 9 triangles, the hexahexaflexagon a ring of 18. The simulation stores where each paper corner sits and the stacking order in each of the six sectors, then splits and reopens the stacks for every flex. The V-flex follows Scott Sherman's definition of it in pat notation, which says how each stack splits and where the pieces go. As a check, every state reached by pinch flexes alone shows whole faces, never a mix, and in the hexahexaflexagon faces 1 to 3 pair with four other faces while 4 to 6 pair with only two.
