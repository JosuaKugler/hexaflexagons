Hexaflexagon Explorer

A hexaflexagon is a hexagon folded from one strip of paper triangles and glued into a loop. Pinch three corners together and it turns inside out, bringing a hidden face to the top. The model below tracks every triangle of the glued strip, so each flex follows the same rules as the paper. Besides the classic pinch flex, the hexahexaflexagon can also do the V-flex, which breaks faces apart and mixes them. The model performs it the way Scott Sherman describes it in Flex Theory: fold two corners together, open the centre, slide, and open up again.

Download the booklet Flexagons: paper that hides its faces (PDF), a hands-on introduction for students: how to fold flexagons, games to play with them, their history, and the mathematics behind them.

What a pinch flex does

The three marked corners rise and meet above the middle, folding the hexagon into a three-bladed star. The blades then split open from the top and lie flat again. The corners you pinched are now the centre, and the old centre has become three of the outer corners.

Why it sometimes won't open

A blade can only split where its layers are not hinged together. If no such gap exists at the corners you chose, the paper refuses. Move one corner over and try again. The Tuckerman tour does this automatically: flex at the same place while it works, otherwise shift by one corner.

What a V-flex does

Scott Sherman's Flex Theory (Gathering 4 Gardner 10) says: fold together opposite vertices, open up the flexagon in the centre, then slide the pats before opening it up again. The model follows the photo sequence on his Flexing Flexagons page: bring two opposite corners together so that each half curls into an open tetrahedron, open the centre into a square pyramid with a pocket at one corner, slide the front wall across so the pocket closes and a new one opens on the other side, close the pyramid so the pocket's tip meets the apex, and open it out from the other side. The nine pieces of Sherman's pattern move as rigid triangles for the fold and for the unfolding at the end, which is the fold run backwards in mirror image. The three moves in between, opening the pyramid, sliding, and closing it again, cannot be done with rigid triangles in this model: the leaves have to give a little, which is the bending Sherman notes for this flex. For each of them the model stops, explains what happens, and shows the position after. Choose V-flex and press the button to step through it. The flex needs the two sectors on either side of the first corner, and one more, to hold at least two leaves each, so the trihexaflexagon can never do it.

Mixed faces

After a V-flex a side can show triangles from several faces, and the pictures on them can turn. With pinch flexes alone the hexahexaflexagon has 9 arrangements, counting each only once however it is rotated or turned over. Pinch and V-flexes together reach 3,420. The simulation finds exactly that number, as 41,040 states (3,420 times 6 rotations and 2 sides), matching Iacob, McLean and Wang (2012).

How the model is built

The trihexaflexagon is a ring of 9 triangles, the hexahexaflexagon a ring of 18. The simulation stores where each paper corner sits and the stacking order in each of the six sectors, then splits and reopens the stacks for every flex. The V-flex follows Scott Sherman's definition of it in pat notation (Flex Theory, Gathering 4 Gardner 10), which says how each stack splits and where the pieces go. As a check, every state reached by pinch flexes alone shows whole faces, never a mix, and in the hexahexaflexagon faces 1 to 3 pair with four other faces while 4 to 6 pair with only two.
